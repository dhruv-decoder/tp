Part 1

Rationale

One page on the four questions in the brief: sources, states, method, presentation.

1. How I chose sources

I started with the obvious plan: Google News RSS, several queries per state (Info/PLAN.md). Two things changed my mind.

  • News describes events, policy researchers need instruments. Ten articles about "Tamil Nadu AI mission" are one decision by one government. If the unit is the article, the hard work (deduplication, finding the date, finding the issuing body) never ends. If the unit is the instrument, ten articles become one record with nine extra links.
  • News search is noisy for this topic. Most "state + AI" headlines are company launches in Bengaluru or Hyderabad, not state action.

So the order of preference is: gazette or the issuing department's own site → PIB or state press release → legislature or court record → reputable news. News is still used, but to find items and to back them up, not as the main source. Every record says which kind of source it rests on (source_type) and how far it was checked (confidence). When the only source was a news-search link, the pipeline forces confidence to "low" automatically.

For keeping it current, the daily scan reads official feeds (PIB, RBI, SEBI, TRAI). Several .gov.in sites refuse requests from cloud servers, so for those the scan uses Google News limited to the official domain (site:pib.gov.in). That is a workaround, and the README says so.

2. Which states, and why

I began with five states that each test something different:

State Why
Karnataka, Telangana, Tamil Nadu Most activity. Tests precision and deduplication
Uttar Pradesh Hindi coverage and name variants ("UP", "U.P.", "Uttar-Pradesh")
Odisha Little coverage. Tests whether the method still finds signal

Once the method was working on these five, AI-assisted research made it cheap to run the same steps for every state and UT. I expanded, but kept the brief's warning in mind: coverage is uneven, and the site says so. The five priority states have 7 to 12 records each and are the best checked. Many small states and UTs have 1 to 3. Confidence labels are shown everywhere so a thin, low-confidence state does not look as solid as Karnataka. Union instruments (178, including 34 international commitments) are included because state policy is often a response to them.

3. Method

  1. Collect. Research by region and topic, written to JSON in a fixed schema (research/.../SCHEMA.md).
  2. Normalise states. Codes and names are mapped to one list of 36 states/UTs plus Union (TS→TG, Orissa→OD, Central→IN...).
  3. Normalise dates. ISO dates, plus date_precision (day 266 / month 25 / year 21) so an approximate date is never shown as exact.
  4. Check values. Type, status, theme and sector must come from fixed lists; aliases are mapped (e.g. "notified" → in-force).
  5. Deduplicate. Same id → merge. Then, within a state, titles that share most words and are close in date are merged; the better-sourced record wins and the other's link goes to secondary_sources. A draft and its final version are never merged; they are linked. Unclear pairs are flagged for a human (16 flagged, 5 merged automatically, 18 dropped by hand in overrides.json).
  6. Lint wording. Summaries with marketing words ("landmark", "robust"...) are flagged and rewritten.
  7. Validate and export to JSON and CSV.

Where I drew the line: a public authority must be the actor and AI must be the subject (or the authority must say the instrument governs AI). In: policies, missions, task forces, centres of excellence, MoUs the state signed, AI deployments a department announced, budget lines, laws, rules, court rulings. Out: company news, speeches, hackathons, think-tank papers.

4. How I present it, and why

The audience is researchers and policymakers. Their first question is "what has my state done?", so the home page is a map, and a click answers that question straight away: a window with the state's numbers and its top 5 instruments, then one button to see the rest. The top 5 follow a simple rule anyone can predict: in force first, then laws and core policies, then better-sourced, then newer.

Each record keeps the details a researcher needs to cite it: issuing body, status, binding or not, exact date precision, source link, and a sentence on what was verified. A table, timeline, state comparison and CSV download serve people who want to work with the data themselves. Copy is kept short and plain on purpose.

Part 2

How it was built

In plain words: what the tool does, the steps taken to build it, and why each choice was made.

Top features

  1. Click a state and see what it has done. A window opens with the state's counts and its top 5 policies, shown one by one. One button opens the full list.
  2. One record per policy, not per news article. Repeated reports of the same announcement are merged into one record; the extra links are kept.
  3. Every record shows how sure we are. "Primary source verified", "Official secondary source" or "Unconfirmed", with a note on what was checked.
  4. Clean states and dates. One spelling per state, and dates that say whether they are exact to the day, the month or only the year.
  5. Status and binding force. Draft vs adopted vs in force, and binding vs advisory, so a press announcement is not mistaken for a law.
  6. Time slider. Watch the map fill in from 2018 to 2026.
  7. Ready to cite and reuse. CSV for the whole dataset, one state or a filtered list; a page per policy with its source and a citation.
  8. Stays current with a human check. A daily scan of official feeds drafts new candidates; a person approves them through a pull request.

Why the top 5 are ranked the way they are

briefingScore in lib/data.ts adds four simple scores:

  • Status: in force or adopted (3) > draft, proposed or in court (1.5) > lapsed or withdrawn (0)
  • Type: law and core policy (3) > institutions and court rulings (2) > guidance (1.5) > consultations and international (1)
  • Source: primary source verified (2) > official secondary (1) > unconfirmed (0)
  • Recency: 0 to 1, newer is higher

So a law in force that we verified ranks above a news report of an announced plan. The rule is shown in the window ("ranked by status, type, source, recency") so nobody mistakes it for a judgement of importance.

Part 3

README

What you can do on the site

  1. Map (home). India shaded by number of policies. Click a state, or pick it from "Go to a state", and a window opens with its top 5 policies, one after another. "See all" opens the full state page. "Union (national)" does the same for central policies.
  2. Filter by type and year. Chips filter by type (law, court, policy, guidance, consultation, institution, international). The slider and play button show how the map filled in from 2018 to now.
  3. State page (/jurisdiction/KA). Every policy for one state, grouped by type, with a CSV download for that state.
  4. Policy page (/policy/<id>). Summary, key provisions, timeline, sources, what was verified, related policies, a citation to copy.
  5. Policies. Search box, three main filters (state, type, status) and more on demand. Results grouped by year, 30 at a time, downloadable as CSV.
  6. Timeline and Compare. Policies over time; two or three states side by side.
  7. News feed. New items from official sources (PIB, RBI, SEBI, TRAI and Google News limited to government sites) that mention AI and may need adding.

Run it locally

Needs Node 20 or later.

npm install
npm run dev          # http://localhost:3000

Data commands:

npm run data:merge     # research JSON -> data/policies.json -> data/policies.csv
npm run data:validate  # schema, duplicate id and broken-link-between-records checks
npm run data:csv       # rebuild data/policies.csv only
npm run scan           # daily scan; needs ANTHROPIC_API_KEY, else it only lists raw matches

Deploy: vercel --prod (project india-ai-policy-atlas).

Schema (one row per instrument)

The brief's minimum fields are all present: state (jurisdiction_name), title, date, source name (issuing_body), source URL, one-line summary, category (instrument_type and family).

Field Meaning
id Stable slug, e.g. ka-gig-workers-act-2025
title Official name of the instrument
jurisdiction, jurisdiction_name Normalised code and name (KA, Karnataka; IN = Union)
issuing_body Department, regulator, legislature or court
instrument_type One of 23 types (act, rules, policy, mission, guidelines, court-ruling, institution…)
family 7 groups of types, used for colour and filters
status in-force, adopted, draft, consultation, proposed, announced, lapsed, withdrawn, superseded…
binding binding, non-binding, mixed
date, date_precision ISO date and whether it is known to the day, month or only year
summary, key_provisions One-line summary and main points
themes, sectors Controlled lists (e.g. deepfakes, skilling; health, agriculture)
source_url, source_type Best source found, and its kind (official, gazette, press-release, legislature, court, news, secondary)
secondary_sources Other reports of the same instrument (where duplicates go)
confidence, confidence_note high / medium / low, and what was or was not verified
related Linked records (an Act and its Rules, a draft and its final)
last_verified When the record was last checked

Assumptions

  • A record is one instrument (a policy, law, mission, institution, MoU-backed programme, court ruling…), not one news article. Ten articles about the same policy are one record with extra links in secondary_sources.
  • A record counts when a public authority is the actor and AI is the subject, or the authority itself says the instrument governs AI. Company news, speeches, hackathons and think-tank papers are out.
  • The date is when the instrument was approved, notified or released, not when it was reported.
  • No record for a state means we found nothing official, not that nothing exists.

Known holes

  • Sourcing differs from the brief's default. Records were compiled from official documents with AI-assisted web research, not from a news API alone. News is used to discover items and stored as secondary sources. See docs/RATIONALE.md.
  • 80 of 312 records are low confidence (a single news report, or a news-search link as the source). They stay visible and labelled; treat them as leads.
  • Not every record was read by a human against the original document. 54 are "primary source verified". The rest rely on official press releases or reputable reports.
  • English first. The scan matches a few Hindi terms (एआई, कृत्रिम बुद्धिमत्ता, डीपफेक). Other regional-language sources are not covered, so smaller states are likely undercounted.
  • Some government sites block automated requests from cloud servers. For those, the scan uses Google News results limited to the official domain (site:pib.gov.in etc.).
  • The daily scan needs setup: an ANTHROPIC_API_KEY secret in the GitHub repo. Without it the scan only lists raw matches, and nothing is added to the dataset without a person approving a pull request.
  • Duplicate detection is word-overlap on titles, plus manual review of flagged pairs. It can miss duplicates that are named very differently.
  • "Top 5" ranking is a simple, visible rule (status, then type, then confidence, then recency). It is a reading aid, not a judgement of importance.
  • Links rot. check-links.mjs reports broken sources weekly; fixes are manual.
  • No hand-labelled accuracy check yet. The first plan included labelling a random sample of about 40 records to measure precision. That was not done.
  • Git history is short (about 12 commits). Much of the data work happened in research sessions and landed in a few large commits.
Part 4

AI use

Short note for the submission (under 200 words)

I used Claude in two ways. In Claude chat I talked through the brief and wrote the first plan: the inclusion rule, the five priority states, the schema, and how to handle duplicates, dates and state names. The ideas on confidence labels, policy status, automatic updates and using Claude to check records were mine; Claude turned them into fields and pipeline steps.

In Claude Code I used it as a build assistant. It wrote most of the code (the Next.js site, the merge script, the daily scan) and helped me scale the research from five states to every state and the Union, running the same method region by region. Its research drafts were not always right: some entries rested on a single news report, and some were duplicates. So the pipeline forces news-only records to low confidence, flags likely duplicates for review, and I dropped 18 records by hand. I also asked it to redesign the home page around a state "briefing" window when the first design was hard to read.

The scope decisions, the line on what counts, and the presentation are mine. Transcripts are in the next tab.

My ideas that shaped the tool

Automatic updates, source reliability (confidence), policy stage (status), handling fake or conflicting reports, local languages, fuzzy matching vs a heavier model, Claude-assisted verification, and a prototype that feels like an assistant: pick a state, get a short briefing, then go deeper. Where each one ended up is in HOW_IT_WAS_BUILT.md.

Where AI got things wrong, and the checks

  • Single-source records. Some drafts relied on one news article. Fix: records whose source is a news-search link are forced to low confidence by the merge script (see _merge-report.txt); 80 records are low and labelled.
  • Duplicates. The same policy was found by different research passes under different names. Fix: automatic merge for close matches, 16 pairs flagged for review, 18 records dropped in overrides.json.
  • Wording. Drafts used marketing words ("landmark", "robust"). Fix: the merge script flags banned words.
  • Not everything is hand-verified. 54 records are marked "primary source verified". The rest say what was and was not checked.
